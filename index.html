Systems rarely fail loudly at first. They drift: a queue that grows a little each night, a certificate nobody renewed, a retry with no ceiling. My work is the part that notices, absorbs and recovers before any of it reaches a person.

Seven years of backend engineering in banking, most of it on platforms other teams depend on and rarely think about. Spring Boot and Python services, Kafka through the middle, Kubernetes underneath, and a strong preference for designs that state their failure behaviour in writing before anyone writes code.

I am least interested in the happy path. The questions I keep coming back to are the unglamorous ones: what happens when the dependency is slow, who finds out, and how does the system get back on its feet without a human in the loop.

II — Operating principle The Shawshank Redemption
Get busy living or get busy dying

Entropy is the default state of everything I have ever been handed. Certificates expire, queues fill, dashboards quietly stop reporting. Engineering is the refusal.

III — Practice

Where the
hours went

01

JPMorgan Chase & Co.

Jul 2026 — Present Current

SDE 2 — core trading platforms, Asset Management

The remit

Building and modernizing the core trading platforms behind Asset Management — scalable, resilient and fast, while the system underneath is replaced piece by piece.

Why it is hard

Trading platforms fail in public. The work is to hold throughput up and keep the failure modes boring, and to make the replacement invisible to the desk that depends on it.

02

Barclays

Aug 2022 — Jul 2026

Software Engineer BA4 — modernization and resilience, at platform scale

Rebuilding the control plane

Global job scheduling ran through legacy command-line tools with no real authorization model and nowhere to grow. I replaced them with a highly available Spring Boot service behind a single REST API.

Authorization became a multi-constraint layer over a Redis cache-aside pattern, wrapped in circuit breakers so a slow dependency costs latency rather than correctness.

Moving alerts faster

Enterprise alerting was losing time to duplication and misrouting at the source. I designed an enrichment and preprocessing engine in Python and Nginx with dynamic, schema-driven routing, so an alert lands where it matters the moment it arrives.

Removing the manual parts

Credential rotation, maintenance suppression and resource onboarding were all human rituals with human failure rates. Each became a workflow instead: Vault-driven restarts across OpenShift, maintenance-aware alert suppression, and an event-driven onboarding path wired into ServiceNow.

What I took from it

Four years on platforms that other teams build on. The lesson that stuck is that the interface is the product — a well-behaved API with honest failure semantics does more for reliability than any amount of internal cleverness.

03

BNP Paribas

Jan 2022 — Jun 2022

DevOps Engineer — containers, pipelines, configuration as code

Scaling the runway

Moved deployments onto Kubernetes and OpenShift and made them scale horizontally through peak volume rather than by hand at the worst possible hour.

Ending configuration drift

Standardized environments in Ansible so drift stopped being a debugging step, and wrote collections that took secret handling out of human hands. Quality and security gates moved into the pipeline, ahead of review rather than after it.

04

Capgemini

Jun 2019 — Dec 2021

Senior Analyst — where the API habits formed

The craft years

RESTful services in Django and Flask, and the long argument for splitting a monolith into pieces that could be reasoned about — and deployed — separately.

The lesson

A backend nobody can observe is a backend nobody can fix. I built real-time dashboards in React and Angular so the people on call could see what I could see, and I have not shipped a service without that instinct since.
