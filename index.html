I build things that have to work at scale — and I build the whole of them. The service, the schema, the pipeline moving data between them, and the interface somebody actually uses. Owning the full path is what makes the design honest.

Seven years across four banks, most of it spent on platforms other teams build on. Spring Boot and Python services, Kafka through the middle, Postgres and Redis holding state, Kubernetes underneath, Angular and React where the work meets a person. The common thread is scale: designs that keep their shape as load, teams and scope grow.

I like problems that span layers, because that is where the good decisions live. A slow page is sometimes a frontend problem and sometimes a missing index; a flaky workflow is sometimes a bug and sometimes a design that never said what it would do under load. Being able to follow the thread the whole way is most of the job.

II — Operating principle Abelson & Sussman · SICP, 1985
For people to read and only incidentally for machines to execute

“Programs must be written for people to read, and only incidentally for machines to execute.” Forty years on, it is still the whole brief — the compiler will forgive almost anything, and the next engineer will forgive nothing.

III — Practice

01

JPMorgan Chase & Co.

Jul 2026 — Present Current

SDE 2 — core trading platforms, Asset Management

What I build

Core trading platforms for Asset Management: the services that carry the flow, the data model underneath them, and the screens the desk works in. Scalable, resilient and fast, engineered to stay that way as volume grows.

The scale of it

Modernization on a platform that cannot stop trading. The craft is delivering real capability every quarter while the system underneath is replaced piece by piece, and making the swap invisible to the people using it.

02

Barclays

Aug 2022 — Jul 2026

Software Engineer BA4 — platform engineering, end to end

A control plane for the whole estate

Global job scheduling ran through legacy command-line tools that could not grow. I designed and built its replacement: a highly available Spring Boot service behind one REST API, with a multi-constraint authorization layer over a Redis cache-aside pattern and circuit breakers holding the edges.

It went from a handful of operators to being the way an entire estate is scheduled — which is the only test of an interface that matters.

Moving events at volume

An enrichment and preprocessing engine in Python and Nginx with dynamic, schema-driven routing, built to take enterprise alert traffic and place each event where it belongs the moment it arrives rather than several hops later.

Products, not scripts

Credential rotation, maintenance suppression and resource onboarding were manual rituals. Each became a product with a front end and an API: Vault-driven restarts across OpenShift, maintenance-aware suppression, and an Angular and Django onboarding platform wired into ServiceNow over events.

What four years taught me

Building for other engineers is its own discipline. The interface is the product — a clear API with honest semantics carries more weight than any amount of internal cleverness, and it is what lets a platform keep growing after you stop touching it.

03

BNP Paribas

Jan 2022 — Jun 2022

DevOps Engineer — delivery, containers, configuration as code

Making room to grow

Moved deployments onto Kubernetes and OpenShift and gave them room to scale horizontally through peak volume, instead of scaling by hand at the worst possible hour.

Delivery as a product

Standardized environments in Ansible, wrote collections that took secret handling out of human hands, and moved quality and security gates into the pipeline ahead of review. Shipping became something the team could do on any given afternoon.

04

Capgemini

Jun 2019 — Dec 2021

Senior Analyst — where the full stack habit started

Both ends of the wire

RESTful services in Django and Flask, the case for splitting a monolith into pieces that could be deployed separately, and the React and Angular front ends that consumed them — usually written by the same person, which is where the habit comes from.

The lesson

A backend nobody can see is a backend nobody can fix. Building the dashboards as well as the services taught me to design the API around the thing someone eventually has to look at.
